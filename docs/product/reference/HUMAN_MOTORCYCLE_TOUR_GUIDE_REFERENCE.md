# Human Motorcycle Tour Guide Reference

Date: 2026-08-04

## Purpose

This is the service-design reference for RideHorizon's passive contextual tour-guide level. It describes the judgement and behaviour of an experienced human guide on a paid small-group motorcycle tour. It is not an implementation specification and it does not expand the current private-beta scope.

## Rider And Experience

The reference rider is travelling through an unfamiliar country or region for pleasure and has deliberately opted into a guided experience. They want to enjoy the road while gaining a sense of the landscape, settlements, history, culture and present-day life around them.

They are buying curated attention, not fact density. At the end of the day, they should feel that they travelled through somewhere rather than merely following turns and covering distance.

## The Guide's Role

The guide combines four roles:

- **Host:** understands the group, sets expectations and adjusts the experience.
- **Road captain:** manages the day's shape, group communication and operational changes.
- **Cultural interpreter:** connects visible places and features to their wider meaning.
- **Group minder:** watches conditions, fatigue, confidence, cohesion and attention.

The governing rule is:

> Safety and riding load decide when the guide may speak. Visible and narrative significance decide what is worth saying.

## Brief To A New Guide

Your job is not to fill the intercom. Help riders notice, understand and enjoy the country while getting them through the day safely and comfortably.

Before the tour, learn the riders' experience, confidence, language, interests, physical needs and expectations. Explain local riding conditions, the route, daily rhythm, group roles, pace, formation, separation procedure, stops and communication discipline. Each rider remains responsible for their own safe decisions.

At the morning briefing, give the shape of the day:

- destination, distance and likely duration;
- road character and difficulty;
- weather, fuel, food and rest plan;
- principal hazards and route options;
- major sights and worthwhile stops; and
- the landscape or human story that ties the day together.

For example: "Today the story is water. We start in the limestone hills, follow the valleys where water powered industry, and finish beside the estuary that opened the region to trade."

Do the routine logistics before departure so they do not clutter the ride.

## Guide Priority

| Priority | Behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate hazard | Give the shortest unambiguous actionable warning. |
| Route or group problem | Explain only what riders need to do. |
| Top visual priority | Identify it before riders reach it, then explain why it matters. |
| Wider place story | Add it only on a calm, predictable section. |
| Interesting but non-essential detail | Save it for a stop or omit it. |
| Nothing worthwhile to add | Stay silent. |

Professional moving-vehicle guides use the term **Top Visual Priority** for the few sights that must be selected, located precisely and indicated while visitors can still see them. They are not expected to mention everything.

## What The Guide Interprets

### Orientation And Transitions

- Country, region, valley, watershed, pass or settlement being entered.
- A visible change in landscape, building style, land use, language or identity.
- How the current place relates to somewhere already visited.
- What will change during the next part of the ride.

An administrative boundary matters only when it provides useful orientation or represents a meaningful change.

### The Visible Scene

Start with the tangible object, locate it precisely, then explain its significance.

> "At the next opening, look left across the valley for the long stone mill buildings. They explain why these settlements grew beside the river rather than on the ridge: flowing water powered the local industry."

### The Road

The road is part of the destination. Explain, where reliable and relevant:

- why it follows a river, ridge, pass or older route;
- who built, used or changed it;
- how geology, climate, engineering or land ownership shaped it; and
- why this section feels distinctive to ride.

This enriches the experience. It is not unsolicited technique or safety coaching.

### Landscape And Nature

Connect geology, rivers, vegetation, wildlife, agriculture, forestry and building materials to what the rider can currently perceive. Identification answers "what?"; interpretation answers "why here?" and "why does it matter?"

### People And Contemporary Life

Include livelihoods, industries, language, food, customs, population change and present-day use of historic places. Do not describe a living country as if it ended two centuries ago.

### Human Stories

Prefer one person, choice or revealing incident over a list of names and dates. Distinguish documented history, local tradition, contested interpretation and the guide's own experience.

## When To Speak

Useful triggers are:

- **Anticipation:** an important view or landmark is about to appear.
- **Transition:** landscape, settlement, road character or cultural context changes.
- **Arrival:** a place begins a new chapter in the journey.
- **Explanation:** something visible naturally raises "why is it like that?"
- **Connection:** a scene completes or contrasts with something explained earlier.
- **Rider interest:** a question or previous reaction reveals what this group values.
- **Spare attention:** the road is open and predictable and the group is settled.

The normal moving pattern is:

1. Locate the subject precisely.
2. Name it.
3. Explain one reason it matters.
4. Connect it to the journey when useful.
5. Release the riders back into the experience.

Guide ahead. "After the next bend, high on the left" is useful. "That building we just passed" is failed timing. If the moment is missed, omit it rather than delivering a stale backlog.

## When To Be Silent

Do not give cultural commentary during complex junctions, overtakes, technical or blind bends, poor surfaces, dense traffic, bad weather or visibility, group separation, navigation uncertainty, an incident or any period of high riding workload.

Silence is also appropriate after a strong view or story and whenever there is no reliable, locally relevant contribution. The road, engine, weather and scenery are part of the experience.

Use stops for longer stories, maps, questions, contested history, local encounters and anything needing sustained attention. Use fuel, coffee and lunch stops to check fatigue, pace, interests and whether the balance of speech and silence is right.

## Sequence And Adaptation

Build the day as a story rather than a feed of independent place entries:

- introduce regional context once;
- use later places for contrast, consequence or a distinct local anchor;
- make callbacks when a later scene completes an earlier explanation;
- avoid repeating the same geology, industry or architectural motif at every settlement;
- notice what engages the group and adapt subject, depth and frequency; and
- close or recap a chapter before introducing the next one.

The guide knows far more than they say. Selection is part of the service.

## Product Implications

The passive tour guide is not achieved by attaching a longer fact to every boundary. It requires:

- a bounded memory of the ride and what has already been explained;
- meaningful events beyond administrative boundary changes;
- visual and narrative priority;
- timing relative to both the scene and riding demand;
- different treatment for moving and stopped riders;
- continuity, contrast and recap; and
- a positive decision to remain silent when no contribution earns the rider's attention.

The unit of value is a well-timed piece of interpretation that makes the present scene more legible, meaningful or memorable.
